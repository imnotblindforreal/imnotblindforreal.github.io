A MAP FOR PEOPLE WHO DO NOT KNOW THE WAY

[1] When a group of secondary-school students volunteered to redesign the visitor map for the fictional town of Bellwick, they expected a straightforward task. The existing map was crowded with symbols, several street names were difficult to read, and the tourist office wanted something more attractive. The students began by choosing colours and drawing neat outlines of the town's main buildings. Their first version looked impressive on a classroom screen. However, when they asked unfamiliar visitors to use it, several people struggled to find a public garden less than ten minutes away. The problem was not a shortage of information. It was the assumption that information obvious to a local resident would be equally obvious to a newcomer.

[2] The original map placed north at the top and represented every street with equal precision. These choices made it look orderly, but they did not necessarily help someone standing outside the railway station. Visitors first needed to identify where they were and which direction they were facing. The students therefore tested a second version on a board beside the station exit. This version was turned so that the streets ahead of the viewer appeared at the top. A large marker showed the viewer's position, and a simple drawing of the clock tower provided a visible point of reference. The students kept north clearly marked, but it no longer determined the entire layout.

[3] Another difficulty concerned the routes themselves. The shortest route on paper passed through a narrow alley and up a flight of steps. It was convenient for some visitors, but unsuitable for anyone pushing a heavy suitcase or using a wheelchair. Instead of marking a single "best route", the students added alternatives and explained the differences. One route was shorter; another avoided steps; a third passed more shops and public toilets. They resisted the temptation to label the step-free route "easy", since a long uphill section might still be demanding. Describing a route's features allowed users to decide for themselves whether it suited their needs.

[4] Selecting landmarks proved equally revealing. Local residents often gave directions using the name of a shop that had closed years earlier. Younger visitors could see the building but had no reason to connect it with that name. The students replaced such references with features that people could actually identify: a striped awning, a stone bridge and a fountain. Yet even visible landmarks had limitations. A market stall might disappear by late afternoon, while a distinctive tree could become less noticeable after dark. The team began to distinguish between permanent landmarks and those that depended on the time of day.

[5] To improve the map, the students stopped explaining it before each trial. Earlier, they had unintentionally helped visitors by pointing out symbols and suggesting where to begin. This made the map seem more successful than it really was. In later trials, they simply handed over a copy and watched. They noted where users hesitated, which labels they searched for and when they turned the paper around. After each walk, visitors were asked what they had expected particular symbols to mean. A symbol intended to represent a viewpoint, for example, was repeatedly mistaken for a camera shop. The students changed it rather than blaming users for interpreting it incorrectly.

[6] The final map contained fewer details than the first version, but each detail had a clearer purpose. It also carried a revision date and invited visitors to report changes, such as a closed entrance or a newly installed crossing. The students recognised that no map could remain useful indefinitely without attention. More fundamentally, they had changed their understanding of the task. A map was not simply a reduced picture of a place. It was a practical guide that had to connect the designer's knowledge with a stranger's decisions. Its success depended less on how much the designer could include than on what the user could confidently do with it.
